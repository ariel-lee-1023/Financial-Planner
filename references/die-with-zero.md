# Die with Zero — Bill Perkins
**Format**: markdown (extracted) | **Pages**: ~240 | **Sections**: 9 rules + appendices | **Depth**: study

**Application boundary:** This file preserves Perkins' spending philosophy, not a universal household objective. Respect intended bequests, dependants, uncertainty, and the user's preferences. The survival-threshold formula is a source illustration, not an actuarial guarantee or sufficient evidence to retire or spend down.

## Mental Model (read first)
Money is life energy converted into experiences. The objective function is **maximize lifetime fulfillment**, not terminal net worth. Health and the capacity to enjoy money both decline with age, so experiences have a hard time window. Unspent money at death is wasted life energy. The nine rules convert this into an actionable optimization problem: time experiences correctly, aim for zero residual wealth (after a survival floor), give while alive, and stop accumulating past the utility peak.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Rule 1 — Maximize your positive life experiences
- **Core idea**: Treat life as a finite optimization problem of fulfillment, not survival or wealth maximization.
- **Life energy** — hours of living converted into money via work; spending money is spending life energy. From *Your Money or Your Life* (Dominguez/Robins), internalized by Perkins.
- **Anti-pattern** — living as if life is infinite: delaying gratification indefinitely so that opportunities expire with health.
- Timing matters more than absolute dollars: an experience at the wrong biological age yields near-zero fulfillment.

### Rule 2 — Invest in experiences early (memory dividend)
- **Core idea**: Experiences are investments that pay a recurring **memory dividend** for the rest of life.
- **Memory dividend**: the ongoing psychological return from past experiences (photos, stories, identity). Early investment compounds because the dividend is collected over more remaining years.
- **Return on experience vs. return on equity**: compare the fulfillment ROI of spending now against the financial ROI of delaying.
- **Anti-pattern** — postponing all discretionary spending until “someday” (retirement), when health curves have already declined.

### Rule 3 — Aim to die with zero
- **Core idea**: Unspent money at death is life energy that produced no fulfillment for you or, if given too late, for heirs.
- **Life-Cycle Hypothesis (Modigliani)**: rational agents should smooth consumption over the lifetime and exhaust resources by the end (under certainty). Perkins operationalizes the real-world version under uncertainty.
- **Anti-pattern** — treating net worth as a high-score to maximize (*Space Invaders* mentality); the ultra-wealthy who cannot give away faster than their capital compounds.

### Rule 4 — Use tools to die with zero (without hitting zero early)
- **Core idea**: Solve the longevity/mortality risk problem so you can spend more aggressively while alive.
- **Annuities** as longevity insurance: convert a stock of capital into a lifetime income stream, removing the need to hold a large precautionary balance.
- **Go-go / slow-go / no-go years**: spending capacity and desire peak in early retirement and fall sharply later; front-load spending accordingly.
- **Final Countdown app / actuarial calculators**: estimate remaining years and required survival capital.
- **Anti-pattern** — pure self-insurance against living to 100 that forces massive underspending in the years of highest utility.

### Rule 5 — Give money to kids and charity when it has maximum impact
- **Core idea**: The utility of a dollar to a recipient is highest when they are younger and need it most, not at the moment of the giver’s death.
- **In-vivo transfers** > bequests for most heirs: give in the 30s–50s of the child’s life rather than the 60s–80s.
- **Probability of inheritance receipt** is random and often late; deliberate timed gifts dominate.
- **Charitable timing**: give while you can still direct and enjoy the impact (contrast with foundations that outlive the donor’s intent).
- **Anti-pattern** — default “they’ll get it when I die” that maximizes neither the giver’s nor the recipient’s fulfillment.

### Rule 6 — Don’t live on autopilot
- **Core idea**: Autopilot (habit of continuous earning/saving without re-evaluation) is the enemy of deliberate experience allocation.
- **50/30/20 and similar rules of thumb** are starting points, not optima; they ignore age-varying utility of money.
- **Personal interest rate**: the utility discount rate applied to future experiences; it **rises with age** because health/energy declines. At advanced ages it can exceed 50–100 %.
- **Anti-pattern** — continuing the same savings rate and work intensity after the point where additional wealth has low marginal utility.

### Rule 7 — Time-bucket your life (distinct seasons)
- **Core idea**: Life is a sequence of non-overlapping windows; some experiences are age-locked and cannot be deferred.
- **Time buckets**: divide remaining life into intervals (e.g., 5–10 year bands) and pre-allocate must-do experiences to the bands in which they are still feasible.
- **Mini-deaths**: each decade closes certain biological and social possibilities; treat the closing of a window as a real loss.
- **Anti-pattern** — a single undifferentiated “bucket list” that ignores sequencing and biological constraints.

### Rule 8 — Know your peak (net-worth peak)
- **Core idea**: Net worth should peak and then decline; continuous accumulation past the utility peak is waste.
- **Declining utility of money with age**: the same dollar buys less fulfillment as health falls.
- **Survival threshold** (floor beneath which you must not fall):
  ```
  Survival threshold ≈ 0.7 × (annual cost of living) × (years of remaining life expectancy)
  ```
  Count home equity only through a feasible, explicitly modelled route to cash or lower housing costs, allowing for timing, terms, and continued housing needs.
- **Know when to stop growing wealth**: once above the survival threshold and past the peak of earning/health capacity, shift from accumulation to deliberate decumulation and experience funding.
- **Anti-pattern** — indefinite wealth maximization that leaves a large unused residue at death.

### Rule 9 — Be bold, not foolish (asymmetric risk when young)
- **Core idea**: The biggest career, geographic, and life risks should be taken when downside is low (youth, few dependents, high recovery time).
- **Asymmetric upside**: early bold moves have limited permanent downside and large upside; late bold moves reverse the asymmetry.
- **Anti-pattern** — extreme risk aversion early (settling for soul-crushing safety) followed by reckless risk late.

### Supporting concepts that cut across rules
- **Fulfillment curve**: fulfillment as a function of spending/experiences; rises then flattens or falls with age/health.
- **Health curves**: biological capacity to enjoy experiences declines; compounding poor health accelerates the decline.
- **Consumption smoothing** (Joe Farrell story): do not transfer money from a poor young self to a rich older self; reverse the usual savings logic when future earning power is high.
- **Phased retirement / go-go years**: design work and spending so that high-utility years are not sacrificed to a pure accumulation phase.

## Worked Example
**Net-worth peak and survival threshold.**  
Client, age 55, annual living cost $80 k, healthy life expectancy ~25 years remaining.  
Survival threshold ≈ 0.7 × 80 k × 25 = $1.4 M.  
Current investable net worth $3.2 M; mortgage $400 k at 3.2 % after-tax; health excellent.  
These figures illustrate why spending now deserves consideration; they do not establish that the client has reached a wealth peak or can safely decumulate. Before recommending drawdown, an annuity, or retaining the mortgage, compare longevity and health-cost scenarios, actual income and liquid assets, dependants, bequests, applicable taxes, and the client's preferences. The $1.4 M heuristic alone cannot authorize the decision.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- If essential spending or catastrophic-risk resilience is insecure, address that before increasing discretionary drawdown. Use a household cash-flow assessment rather than treating the source formula as the boundary between safe and unsafe.
- A low after-tax debt cost, including the source's ~4% reference point, can favor retention when liquidity is valuable. Compare actual terms, risk, cash flow, and preferences before choosing repayment or retention.
- Age-locked experiences can make delay costly. Use the personal-interest-rate concept to discuss that preference; do not invent a numerical utility premium from age alone.
- Compare lifetime gifts with bequests based on recipient needs, the donor's security, legal/tax constraints, and intended timing; preserve the user's chosen legacy goals.
- Once net worth is clearly above the survival threshold and health is still high, the burden of proof shifts to *why continue accumulating*.
- Bold career/life moves are highest-ROI when recovery time is long and downside is bounded; reverse the bias as age and obligations rise.

## Key Takeaways
1. Maximize lifetime fulfillment, not terminal wealth; unspent money is wasted life energy.
2. Experiences compound via the memory dividend — invest early.
3. Time-bucket life; many experiences are biologically time-limited.
4. Know the survival threshold and the net-worth peak; plan to decumulate after the peak.
5. Personal interest rate rises with age; use it to weight present vs. future utility.
6. Give while alive when the recipient’s utility is highest.
7. Be bold when young; the risk/reward asymmetry flips later.
